# Teaching a small language model to read supply-chain news: the project, start to finish

A guide to this project for interviews: what it is for, the data, how the evaluation was built and checked, the
reward and metrics, the tools, what went wrong, and how the project changed over time. All numbers come from the
repository's tests, its README, or measured runs.

---

## 1. The project in 30 seconds

> I built a reinforcement-learning environment that simulates a pharmaceutical warehouse for 60 days: five drugs,
> uncertain demand, uncertain delivery times, and supply disruptions. My goal is to make a **small language model
> (Phi-4-mini, 3.8B)** good at one thing a fixed rule can't do: **reading news about upcoming disruptions and deciding
> how much extra safety stock to keep, and for which drug.**
>
> I made the environment trustworthy first: reproducible seeds, a train/test split, a floor, a baseline and an
> oracle, and paired statistics. Then I tested small models and found they copy numbers instead of reasoning, so I
> redesigned the action so a rule does the arithmetic and the model only makes the judgement call. I found and
> closed a shortcut that let a non-reading policy look smart. Then I designed a GRPO reward that scores each answer
> by simulating 15 days ahead in 4 shared futures. The first GRPO run, on Kaggle, is in progress.

## 2. Purpose and objective

**The real-world problem.** A hospital or pharmacy warehouse must keep critical medicines in stock. Running out of
insulin is far worse than running out of vitamins. Two things make it hard:
- Demand and delivery times are random.
- Disruptions (a strike, a plant fire, an epidemic wave) are often **announced in text before they hit**: a supplier
  warning, a health-authority notice.

A classic inventory rule can't read that text. A language model can.

**Why a small model.** A 3–4B model runs on a laptop (Ollama) or a single cheap GPU, keeps data private, and is
cheap enough to call every day. The question is whether such a model can be **trained** to make this decision well,
not just prompted.

**The objective, stated precisely.** Fine-tune Phi-4-mini with reinforcement learning (GRPO) so that, on **test seeds
it never saw**:
1. it beats the untrained Phi-4-mini, with a 95% confidence interval above zero, **and**
2. it beats **"+3 extra days on every product"**, a fixed habit that reads nothing. Beating it shows the model reads
   the news, not just stocks more.

**What the project also serves as:** an OpenEnv-compliant benchmark (a server with a web playground and a live
dashboard), so other agents and models can be tested on the same tasks.

## 3. The data

### There is no external dataset: the data is generated by a simulator

All data comes from the environment (`server/my_env_environment.py`, `tasks.py`, `news.py`). This was a deliberate
choice. A simulator gives unlimited, labelled-by-outcome data. Any action can be scored by playing it forward, and
any situation can be replayed.

**One episode** = 60 days, 5 products (SKUs):

| SKU | Stockout penalty (importance) | Base demand / day | Base lead time | Storage |
|---|:---:|---|---|---|
| insulin | 100 | 8–12 | 4–6 days | cold |
| bp_medication | 60 | 40–60 | 2–4 days | ambient |
| hydroxychloroquine | 35 | 20–40 | 5–8 days | ambient |
| paracetamol | 20 | 180–220 | 1–3 days | ambient |
| vitamins | 5 | 60–90 | 1–2 days | ambient |

- **Randomness:** each episode's base demand, lead times and disruption sizes are drawn from a **seed**.
  - Daily demand has noise of 12% of base demand.
  - Lead times have noise of 0.75 days.
- **Three tasks:**
  - supply-chain breakdown: delivery delays for two SKUs, one after the other;
  - flu season: a demand surge plus a delay;
  - epidemic in two waves: two surges, and delays.
- **Two scenarios:**
  - **fixed:** the same SKUs and days in every seed; the original benchmark;
  - **random:** which SKUs are disrupted, and when, are drawn per seed; storage holds 14 days of demand instead of
    21. Training uses this one (section 6 explains why).

### The news (the text the model must read)

`news.py` turns each disruption into a news item published **5–10 days before it starts**. It stays visible until the
disruption ends, so about **43 of the 60 days** have at least one item. There are three levels:
- **Level 0:** no news.
- **Level 1:** an exact template.
- **Level 2:** varied wording, approximate timing, severity in words, and generic product names. For example:

> *Day 6: Supply alert: a truck drivers' strike is expected to hit hydroxychloroquine deliveries in about 5 days. New
> orders could be noticeably slower than usual for about 9 days.*

The news never changes the world. A rule that ignores it scores exactly the same with or without it.

### The training data for GRPO

- **One training example = one news day of one episode:** the chat prompt the model would see that day (the system
  prompt, the inventory report and the last 3 days' results).
- **Size:** about 2,000–2,600 tokens per prompt.
- **How a day's state is rebuilt:** reset the episode and let the baseline play every earlier day, so the same
  (task, seed, day) always gives exactly the same prompt.
- **Volume:** 300 training episodes give 12,969 news-day prompts, built in 2 seconds.
- **Filter:** days where every reference answer (+0, +2, +4, +7, +10 days) scores within 0.25 points are dropped,
  because all 8 sampled answers would get the same reward and teach nothing.

## 4. How the project evolved

The order matters for interviews: each phase answered a problem the previous one exposed.

### Phase 0 (April–May): a first environment

A first OpenEnv environment and an `inference.py` script that asked an LLM for orders. It looked complete, but it was
not yet a reliable benchmark.

### Phase 1 (late September): making it a real benchmark

An audit found bugs that made results meaningless:
- **Every task scored 0.01:** `inference.py` sent a Python object the client could not encode, so every reset failed.
- **A crash on any capitalised name:** ordering "Insulin" raised an error and killed the step.
- **Lost deliveries:** two deliveries of the same SKU on the same day were merged into one.
- **An information leak:** the true lead time was visible at order time instead of on delivery.

Fixed all of them, made tasks reproducible from a seed, and added tests. That's where the test suite started; it's at
190 tests now.

### Phase 2 (early October): a reward and an evaluation you can trust

- **Doing nothing scored 0.40:** the old score counted every day without deliveries as "waste-free". The score
  became simply **the average daily reward**, so ordering nothing now scores about 0.
- **Waste** became the weighted share of deliveries rejected because storage was full.
- **Storage** was sized from demand (21 days), and insulin moved to cold storage.
- **A fairness bug:** the noise came from one random generator, so *what the agent ordered changed future demand*.
  Two agents on the same seed faced different worlds. Now all noise is drawn once at reset, and the same seed gives
  the same world whatever the agent does. This is also what makes the RL reward possible later: several candidate
  answers can be replayed in the same future.
- **An evaluation harness** (`eval.py`):
  - a floor (order nothing), a baseline (an order-up-to rule) and an oracle (perfect foresight);
  - a fixed **train/test seed split** (0–9,999 / 10,000–19,999);
  - per-seed paired comparisons with 95% intervals.

### Phase 3 (early October): small models fail in an instructive way

Ran Qwen 2.5 1.5B/3B, Gemma 2 2B and Phi-4-mini locally with Ollama. They **copied numbers instead of reasoning**:

| Answer format | What the model writes | What went wrong | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| `units` | units to order | Qwen 1.5B ordered `{"insulin": 100}` every day (the prompt's example); Gemma copied the shelf counts as order sizes | 0.00 / 0.42 |
| `days` | days of stock wanted (code does the maths) | the models copied `days_of_cover` (what they already had), and stock ran down to nothing | 0.52–0.75 |
| **`adjust`** | **extra days, 0 to +10, on top of the baseline's order** | Phi-4-mini: valid JSON every day, no collapse | **0.886** (baseline 0.875) |

**The design lesson:** let code do the arithmetic and give the model only the judgement, in a format where the worst
answer (0, a copied number, broken JSON) plays like the baseline. Negative values were tested and removed: "−3 days
everywhere" scored 0.728.

The reward was also **weighted by importance**: a day without insulin now costs 100/220 of the day, and a day without
vitamins 5/220. The prompt already told the model to protect important products, so the reward now agrees with it.

### Phase 4 (October): give the model something only it can use

The baseline already reaches about 93% of the oracle from the numbers alone. So I added **news**: information the
baseline can't use, but a language model can. Two references bound what news is worth:
- **Perfect news reading** (`baseline_news`): reads the hidden exact event and adds a formula's extra days. It closes
  56% of the baseline-to-oracle gap at level 1.
- **The oracle:** 100%.

Phi-4-mini with level-2 news: **+0.007 ± 0.016** over the baseline, which is not significant. It picked the right
product and roughly the right timing, but the wrong amounts:
- +5 days where about +14 was needed;
- +10 to +19 days of paracetamol after the flu peak, which wasted thousands of units.

### Phase 5 (early November): finding a shortcut in my own benchmark

Before training, I checked whether a policy could look smart **without reading**:
- A "calendar" (the average perfect-news answer per task, day and product, learned from train seeds) earned **94%**
  of perfect news reading's gain without reading anything, because each task always disrupted the same products on
  the same days.
- Randomising only the days didn't help (98%): the shortcut was mainly *which product*, and with 21 days of storage,
  stockpiling was nearly free.
- **The fix: the random scenario.** The disrupted products are random too, and storage is tighter (14 days). The
  calendar now earns only 50%.

Measured on 50 test seeds per task, level-2 news:

| Agent | Score |
|---|:---:|
| Baseline | 0.859 |
| Calendar (no reading) | 0.868 |
| +3 days on every product (no reading) | 0.869 |
| Perfect news reading | 0.877 |
| Oracle | 0.925 |

Perfect reading beats the best non-reading habit by **+0.008 ± 0.003**. The margin is small but real, and it can only
be earned by reading.

### Phase 6 (November): designing the RL reward, and a check before training

- **A choice:** first build a "teacher" for supervised fine-tuning, or go straight to GRPO? I chose GRPO, because the
  simulator gives exact rewards and the teacher (the formula) is itself far from the oracle.
- **The GRPO reward**, with every parameter chosen by measurement (section 7).
- **A diversity check before spending GPU time** (`local_llm/diversity_check.py`): GRPO only learns from differences
  between the answers to one prompt. At temperature 1.0, Phi-4-mini gave 6–8 different answers per prompt, with
  different rewards, and no broken JSON. So: enough variety.
- **An exploration gap:** on one day the news said "amlodipine" (a blood-pressure drug). The best answer was +7 to
  +10 days of `bp_medication`, worth +5 to +7 points, and **none of 16 samples tried it**. The model didn't know
  amlodipine = `bp_medication`, and GRPO can't reinforce an answer the model never samples. I changed the news to
  generic names ("blood-pressure medication"), because mapping drug names to SKUs is a separate skill from judging
  disruptions.

### Phase 7 (November): the training pipeline on Kaggle

A notebook (`training/grpo_phi4mini_kaggle.ipynb`) that runs everything end to end:
- Phi-4-mini in 4-bit with LoRA (Unsloth) and TRL's `GRPOTrainer`;
- health metrics, a probe exam on every checkpoint, and an automatic time budget;
- the evaluation against every reference, and a written verdict.

With two T4 GPUs, training runs on one, and all evaluation runs on the other in a background process.

## 5. How the evaluation was built and checked

### Principles

1. **Train and test never mix.**
   - Seeds 0–9,999 are for training and tuning; 10,000–19,999 are for reporting.
   - Seeds 9,000–9,099 are kept out of training for the probe exam.
   - Tests assert all of this.
2. **The same seed gives the same world whatever the agent does.** A test checks it: ordering insulin on day 0 must
   not change paracetamol demand later. That makes comparisons fair, and lets any situation be replayed.
3. **Compare seed by seed (paired).** Each agent plays exactly the same episodes. Per-seed differences cancel each
   seed's luck: this gave a confidence interval about 4× tighter than comparing two averages.
4. **A ladder of references** on the same seeds, each answering one question:

| Reference | Answers the question |
|---|---|
| Order nothing (≈ 0.00) | does the score punish doing nothing? |
| Baseline rule | what do numbers alone achieve? (the model answering 0 = exactly this) |
| **+3 days everywhere** | is the model reading, or just stocking more? (**the key bar**) |
| Perfect news reading | what is perfect reading worth? |
| Oracle (knows the future) | the ceiling; "gap closed" = share of baseline-to-oracle distance recovered |

5. **Report uncertainty.** Every result is a mean ± 95% interval. A result counts as real only if mean − interval
   > 0. For example, Phi-4-mini's +0.007 ± 0.016 is reported as **not significant**.
6. **Rescore old runs under new rules.** Every LLM episode saved the exact order sent each day. Because seeds fix the
   world, replaying those orders under the current reward reproduces the run exactly, so results from different
   months stay comparable.

### The probe exam (watching training)

- **What it is:** 100 fixed news days from held-out seeds, picked evenly across the 3 tasks. The model answers
  greedily (no randomness), and each answer is scored with the training reward.
- **"% of best":** total reward ÷ the total of the best reference answer per day. Answering 0 everywhere scores
  exactly 0%.
- **Behaviour checks:**
  - **right product:** the largest extra goes to a product in the news;
  - **insulin habit:** adding insulin when the news isn't about insulin;
  - **held back:** answering 0 on days where every +N loses.

### The final evaluation (after training)

- **Test probe:** 100 test-seed days, base vs trained, paired.
- **Full episodes:** 30 sixty-day episodes (10 test seeds × 3 tasks), random scenario, level-2 news. Trained vs base,
  baseline, "+3 everywhere", perfect reading and the oracle.
- **Safety checks:**
  - **news level 0:** with nothing to read, the model should play like the baseline;
  - **fixed scenario:** it should do no worse than the base model.

### How the evaluation code itself was checked

- **Unit tests:**
  - the batched episode runner plays exactly like the normal LLM agent;
  - answering `{}` scores the same as the baseline;
  - the reward is deterministic, and answering 0 scores exactly 0.
- **A full dry run of the notebook on CPU,** with stand-ins for the model and trainer, in both one-GPU and two-GPU
  mode, including a background worker probing checkpoints.

## 6. Why the random scenario matters (a good interview story)

An evaluation can be fooled. A model that memorised "in the flu task, stock paracetamol around day 30" would score
like a news reader on the fixed tasks without reading anything. I measured this with a non-reading calendar policy
before training a single model. That's how I knew the benchmark had to change: training on the fixed tasks would
have rewarded memorisation. The lesson: **try to beat your own benchmark with a cheating policy before trusting it.**

## 7. Loss, reward and metrics

### The environment's reward (per day) and the score (per episode)

```
step_reward  = Σ stockout_penalty_i × fill_i / Σ stockout_penalty_i  −  waste_fraction_today
episode score = clip( mean of step rewards over 60 days , 0, 1 )
```

- **fill_i** is the share of the day's demand for SKU i that was served.
- **waste** is the share of deliveries rejected because storage was full, weighted by each SKU's waste penalty.

### The GRPO reward (per answer)

```
reward = ( S(answer) − S(answer 0) ) × 100 / 15   +   format penalty

S(x) = mean over 4 futures of the sum of the next 15 daily rewards,
       when today's order uses the answer's extra days and the baseline orders afterwards
```

| Choice | Value | Why (measured on 90 news-day states) |
|---|---|---|
| Horizon | 15 days | best signal-to-noise: 8 days 4.6, **15 days 5.2**, 30 days 4.4 |
| Futures | 4, shared by all 8 answers to a prompt | the best answer matches the 16-future best on 91% of states (the single real future: 74%) |
| Answer applies | today only | holding it longer changes the gain by under 10%, and matches how the model is used |
| Units | points = hundredths of a day's reward, per day | answering 0 scores exactly 0; positive = better than doing nothing extra |
| Penalties | no JSON −1; out of range −0.25 | smaller than a typical good answer, so the decision matters more than the format |

**Shared futures** are the key trick. Luck that hits one answer hits all eight, so the differences between answers
reflect the answers themselves, not the dice.

### The GRPO loss

For each prompt the model samples 8 answers (temperature 1.0), and each answer's **advantage** is its reward minus
the group's mean. The loss raises the probability of above-average answers and lowers the others. A **KL penalty**
(β = 0.04) keeps the model close to the original.

I use **Dr. GRPO** (`loss_type="dr_grpo"`, `scale_rewards=False`): advantages are **not divided by the group's
standard deviation**. When all 8 answers score nearly the same, dividing by that tiny spread would turn noise into
large updates. Without the division, the push stays in reward points.

### Metrics during training

| Metric | Meaning | Healthy |
|---|---|---|
| reward (and 20-step average) | mean reward of the sampled answers | rising slowly |
| groups with no spread | prompts where all 8 answers scored the same | low (these teach nothing) |
| all-zero answers | the model adds nothing | not heading to 100% (that would be collapse) |
| no JSON / out of range | format errors | near 0 |
| right product | the largest extra goes to a product in the news | rising |
| KL | distance from the original model | small, growing slowly |
| completion length | tokens per answer | short and stable |
| **probe exam** | the same 100 held-out days, greedy, every 25 steps | % of best rising above step 0 |

### The final success criteria

Trained − base > 0 with the 95% interval above 0, **and** trained > "+3 everywhere". Also: the news-0 score close to
the baseline, and the fixed scenario not worse than base.

## 8. The model and the training setup

- **Model:** Phi-4-mini-instruct (3.8B), loaded in **4-bit** (~2.5 GB instead of ~7.6 GB).
- **LoRA (QLoRA):**
  - the 3.8B weights stay frozen;
  - small trainable "correction" matrices are added to the attention and MLP layers (rank 16, ~23M parameters, ~0.6%
    of the model);
  - the adapter file is ~45 MB.
- **Why LoRA:**
  - a full fine-tune needs about 45 GB of GPU memory; a T4 has 16 GB;
  - the KL reference model comes for free (the same model with the adapter switched off);
  - it protects what the model already knows (JSON, English).
- **GRPO settings:** 8 answers per prompt, 2 prompts per step, learning rate 1e-5, β 0.04, temperature 1.0, answers up
  to 96 tokens.
- **Hardware:** Kaggle, two T4 GPUs. GPU 0 trains. GPU 1 runs a background worker that evaluates the base model, takes
  a probe exam on every checkpoint, and plays half of the final episodes.
- **Time budget:** Kaggle stops a session after 12 h. A training callback measures seconds per step and stops
  training early, so the evaluation always fits.

## 10. What went wrong, and what I learned

| Problem | How it showed up | Fix | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| The benchmark was broken | every task scored 0.01 | fixed the serialisation, sanitised orders, wrote tests | test the harness before trusting any score |
| Doing nothing scored 0.40 | the old score counted no-delivery days as "waste-free" | score = mean daily reward | the score must measure exactly what is rewarded |
| The agent's orders changed the random future | the same seed gave different worlds | all noise drawn once at reset, plus a test | fairness first; it also made the RL reward possible |
| Small models copied numbers | Qwen ordered 100 insulin every day; Gemma copied shelf counts | the `adjust` format: code does the maths, the model only judges | design the action so the worst answer is safe |
| `days_of_cover` was copied | stock ran down to zero | removed it from the `adjust` prompt | small models copy whatever number looks like an answer |
| Phi-4-mini overreacted to news | +10 to +19 days of paracetamol after the flu peak; worse than baseline on flu | → the motivation for RL instead of prompting | prompting alone gets the direction right, not the size |
| A shortcut in the benchmark | a non-reading calendar earned 94% of the reading gain | random scenario + tighter storage (calendar: 50%) | attack your own benchmark with a cheating policy |
| An exploration gap | the model never sampled `bp_medication` when the news said "amlodipine" | generic product names in the news | RL can only reinforce what the model already sometimes does |
| Kaggle install broke Unsloth | "_imaging extension built for another version of Pillow" | pinned Pillow to the version already loaded | my first fix checked the disk, but the problem was in memory: check the actual state, not the assumed one |
| Training is slow | ~250 s per GRPO step on a T4 (300 steps would need 20 h) | an automatic time budget; GPU 1 does the evaluation | measure speed early; plan around the hardware |
| My own estimate was wrong | I said ~14 of 60 days have news; it is ~43 (news stays visible until the disruption ends) | re-measured before relying on it | check a number before building a plan on it |

## 11. Decisions I made, and why

- **Storage of 21 days and insulin in cold storage** (fixed tasks): realistic, and it fits the best possible plan
  with 25% to spare.
- **No holding cost:** the waste penalty already punishes overstock.
- **Keep the nearly empty starting stock:** every agent loses the first days the same way.
- **The `adjust` action limited to 0..+10, no negatives:** negatives only added stockouts.
- **Reward weighted by stockout penalty:** it matches the instructions and real-world importance.
- **The random scenario:** to remove the memorisation shortcut.
- **GRPO without a supervised warm-up first:** the simulator gives exact rewards, and the formula teacher closes only
  27% of the gap to the oracle in the random scenario.
- **Generic product names rather than a longer prompt:** keep the task about judgement, not vocabulary.

## 12. Where the first training run stands (live numbers)

**Mechanics: healthy.**
- Valid JSON on 94–100% of answers, ~45 tokens each.
- Every group of 8 answers had a spread of rewards (no wasted steps).
- KL ~0.0005: the model is moving, slowly.

**Speed:** ~250 s per step, so training will stop by itself at about step 130 of the planned 300 to leave time for
the evaluation.

**Probe exam** (100 held-out days, greedy):

| | Reward | % of best | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained (step 0) | −0.120 | −12.9% | |
| Step 25 | −0.194 | −20.8% | all-zero 85%, insulin habit 8%, right product 60%, held back 94% |

The difference is within noise: there's no measurable change yet. On most days the greedy model adds nothing, and
when it acts it's often wrong. The open question for the rest of the run is whether greedy answers start acting
correctly on the days that matter.

**Honest expectation:** with ~130 slow, small steps, the likely result is "trained ≈ base ≈ baseline". The run will
still show exactly what to change next.

## 13. What I would do next

1. **Learn faster per step:** a higher learning rate, since the KL is tiny and there's room to move.
2. **Cheaper steps:** shorter prompts (the report and history make ~2,600 tokens, repeated for every one of the 8
   answers), or faster generation (vLLM), so more steps fit in 12 hours.
3. **If the model still never tries large answers:** a short supervised warm-up from the formula teacher, so GRPO has
   good answers to reinforce.
4. **Harder news:** false alarms and irrelevant news (a planned level 3), so "always react" stops working.

## 14. Likely interview questions, with short answers

**Why build a simulator instead of using a dataset?**
Because the right answer isn't in any dataset. Whether +7 days of insulin was a good idea depends on what happens
next. A simulator lets me score any answer by playing it forward, replay situations, and generate unlimited data. The
cost is realism, so I measure against strong references rather than claiming real-world accuracy.

**How do you know your evaluation isn't fooling you?**
- train/test seed split;
- worlds that don't depend on the agent's actions;
- paired comparisons with 95% intervals;
- a ladder of references from "order nothing" to the oracle;
- and above all, I tried to beat my benchmark with non-reading policies (the calendar, "+3 everywhere"), and changed
  the benchmark when they worked too well.

**Why GRPO and not PPO or supervised fine-tuning?**
- **vs PPO:** GRPO needs no value network: the baseline is the group's mean reward, which is simple and cheap on one
  GPU.
- **vs supervised fine-tuning:** my reward is exact (simulation), while the only available teacher is a formula that
  recovers just 27% of the gap to the oracle. Imitating it would cap the model at the teacher's level.

**Why 4 shared futures?**
Each answer is judged against the same random futures, so the differences between answers come from the answers. 4
futures pick the same best answer as 16 futures 91% of the time, at a quarter of the cost.

**Why Dr. GRPO (no std scaling)?**
On days where all answers are nearly equal, dividing by the tiny spread would amplify noise into big updates. Without
scaling, the update size stays proportional to the real difference in points.

**What does "% of best" mean, and why can it be negative?**
It's the total reward on the probe days ÷ the total of the best reference answer. 0% = always answering "no extra
days" (the baseline). It's negative when the model's actions lose points on average, which is the case for the
untrained model.

**What was the hardest bug?**
The randomness bug. One shared random generator meant that what the agent ordered changed future demand. Every
comparison between agents was subtly unfair, and nothing crashed. A test that asserts "same seed, same world,
whatever the agent does" now guards it.

**What would you do differently?**
Attack the benchmark with cheating policies much earlier, before running any LLM on it. And measure training speed
on the target GPU before planning the run.
